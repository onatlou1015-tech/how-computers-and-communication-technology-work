Applying Knowledge to Solve Basic Problems

Troubleshooting common issues, choosing the right device, recommending upgrades, and solving real-life technology problems.

Using System Knowledge to Solve Problems

In the earlier topics, you learned how hardware, software, and networks work. Now you will learn how to use that knowledge to solve real problems. This is called troubleshooting — finding out why something is not working and fixing it.

Troubleshooting is the process of identifying a problem, finding its cause, and applying a solution. When you understand how a computer system works, you can figure out which part is causing the problem and fix it step by step.

Why Understanding the System Matters

Most computer problems have a clear cause. By understanding how the parts work together, you can narrow down where the problem is. For example:

  • If a computer is slow, the problem might be RAM (not enough working memory), storage (full hard drive), or too many background programs running.
  • If there is no internet, the problem might be the router, the cables, the Wi-Fi password, or the service provider.
  • If a printer is not printing, the problem might be the connection, the drivers, or the paper.

A Simple Troubleshooting Method

When you face any technology problem, follow these steps:

  1. Identify the symptom: What exactly is not working? Describe it clearly.
  2. List possible causes: Which parts could be causing this? (Hardware, software, network, or user error?)
  3. Check the simplest causes first: Is it plugged in? Is it turned on? Is the Wi-Fi password correct?
  4. Test one thing at a time: Change only one thing, then check whether the problem is solved.
  5. Record the solution: Once fixed, write down what you did so you can solve the same problem faster next time.

Analogy: Troubleshooting is like being a detective. The symptom is the "crime", and the cause is the "suspect". You look at the evidence, eliminate possibilities, and follow the clues until you find the culprit — and then you fix it.

Troubleshooting Common Problems

Let us look at three common problems that people face with computers and networks. For each one, we will see what the symptom is, what could cause it, and what steps you can take to fix it.

Problem 1

Computer is running slowly

Possible causes and checks:

  • RAM is full: Too many programs open at once. Close unused apps and browser tabs.
  • Background apps: Programs running in the background use up resources. Check Task Manager (Windows) or Activity Monitor (Mac).
  • Storage is almost full: When a drive is nearly full, the computer slows down. Delete or move unused files.
  • Malware: Viruses can slow down a system. Run an antivirus scan.
  • Old hardware: An older CPU or a slow hard drive (HDD) can limit performance. Consider upgrading to an SSD.
Problem 2

No internet connection

Possible causes and checks:

  • Router is off or frozen: Check that the router is on, then restart it by unplugging for 30 seconds.
  • Cables are loose: Check all the cables between the modem, router, and wall.
  • Wi-Fi password: Make sure you are connecting to the correct network with the correct password.
  • Wi-Fi is too far: Move closer to the router or access point.
  • Service outage: The ISP might have a problem in your area. Check with your provider.
Problem 3

Printer is not printing

Possible causes and checks:

  • Not connected: Check that the printer is connected by cable or Wi-Fi and turned on.
  • Drivers missing: The computer needs printer software (drivers). Reinstall or update them.
  • Paper or ink: Check that there is paper in the tray and ink in the cartridges.
  • Print queue stuck: A previous print job might be blocking the queue. Cancel it and try again.
  • Wrong printer selected: Make sure the correct printer is selected before printing.

Applying the Troubleshooting Method

Notice that in each case, the troubleshooting steps follow the same pattern: identify the symptom, list the possible causes, check the simplest things first, and fix the actual cause. This approach works for almost every technology problem you will meet.

Choosing the Right Device for a Task

Not every device is suited for every task. A device that is perfect for writing documents might be a bad choice for professional video editing. When you know what matters for each task, you can recommend the right device.

Task Best Device Why It Works Best
Video editing Powerful desktop or high-end laptop Needs a fast CPU, plenty of RAM (16 GB or more), a dedicated GPU for rendering, and fast SSD storage. Desktops are easier to upgrade.
Gaming Gaming PC or gaming laptop Needs a strong GPU, fast CPU, at least 8–16 GB of RAM, and fast storage. Cooling is important to keep performance high.
Schoolwork Laptop, tablet, or Chromebook Needs good battery life, a reliable keyboard, and enough RAM for documents, browsing, and video calls. Portability matters.
Communication (chat, calls, email) Smartphone, tablet, or laptop Needs good network connectivity, a working camera and microphone, and enough speed for video calls.
Music and video streaming Smart TV, tablet, or smartphone Needs a good screen and speakers, plus a fast enough internet connection to stream without buffering.
Simple web browsing and email Any basic laptop, tablet, or Chromebook A light device with modest RAM and storage is enough. Simpler devices are also cheaper and easier to maintain.

How to Decide

When choosing a device, ask yourself these three questions:

  1. What is the main task? The device must handle the most demanding thing you need it for.
  2. How portable does it need to be? A desktop is more powerful, but a laptop or tablet is easier to carry.
  3. What is the budget? You can often get a good device for less if you choose the right specifications for the task.

Recommending Upgrades or Solutions

Sometimes the problem is not something you can fix by changing settings — the device may simply need better hardware or a better connection. In those cases, you can recommend an upgrade.

Need more speed?

Add RAM or an SSD

Adding more RAM lets the computer handle more programs at once. Replacing an old spinning hard drive with a solid-state drive (SSD) makes the computer start faster and open apps much more quickly.

Need better connectivity?

Upgrade router or internet plan

An old router may not support the newest Wi-Fi standards or the highest speeds. A newer router and a faster internet plan can dramatically improve your experience — especially for video calls, streaming, and gaming.

Need more space?

Add storage

If the drive is full, the computer slows down and cannot store new files. You can add an external drive, install a larger internal drive, or use cloud storage for extra space.

Before You Recommend an Upgrade

Always start by checking whether a simpler fix will solve the problem:

  • Could the issue be solved by closing unnecessary programs instead of buying more RAM?
  • Could cleaning up old files free enough storage space?
  • Could moving the router to a better location improve the Wi-Fi signal?

Only recommend an upgrade if the simpler fixes do not solve the problem. That way, the person saves money and gets the right solution for their real need.

Analogy: Recommending an upgrade is like advising someone on a car. If their car is slow, the fix might be to clean the engine (a simpler fix), or it might be to add a turbocharger (a real upgrade). You always check the simple fix first — and only recommend a bigger change if the simple fix is not enough.

Real-Life Scenarios

The best way to test your knowledge is to apply it to real situations. Below are three common real-life technology scenarios. For each one, think about what the people need and what system would work best for them.

Scenario A

Video calling relatives abroad

"A family wants to video call relatives who live overseas. What do they need?"

What they need:

  • A device with a camera, microphone, and screen — smartphone, tablet, laptop, or computer.
  • A reliable internet connection — Wi-Fi at home, or mobile data if travelling.
  • A video calling app — such as WhatsApp, Zoom, Google Meet, or FaceTime.
  • An account on the same app so both sides can connect.
  • A well-lit, quiet location for the call.
Scenario B

Student needs device for online classes

"A student needs a device for online classes. What should they choose?"

What to look for:

  • A laptop or tablet — easier to type and use than a phone for long classes.
  • At least 4–8 GB RAM and a modern processor for smooth video calls.
  • A good webcam and microphone for interacting with the teacher.
  • Adequate storage for notes, homework, and files.
  • Reliable Wi-Fi connection at home.
  • Battery life of at least 6–8 hours for a full school day.
Scenario C

Small shop accepting digital payments

"A small shop wants to accept digital payments. What system is needed?"

What the system needs:

  • A payment terminal (card reader) or a smartphone with a payment app.
  • A reliable internet connection — Wi-Fi or mobile data — so payments can be processed.
  • A point-of-sale (POS) app or software that tracks sales and totals.
  • A bank account or payment provider account (like PayPal or a local service).
  • A way to print or send receipts to customers — small printer or email receipt.
  • Security measures — a secure connection and safe handling of customer data.

Analysing the Scenarios

Notice that all three scenarios involve the same four ingredients: hardware, software, connectivity, and purpose. When you think about a real-world problem, ask:

  • What hardware is needed?
  • What software is required?
  • What kind of network or connection is needed?
  • What is the purpose or task the system must perform?

When you can answer all four questions, you can design a solution that fits the real need.

Activity 2 — Problem-Solving Stations

Your teacher will set up several stations around the classroom. Each station presents a real-life problem. Work in small groups and rotate through the stations. For each one, discuss and write down your diagnosis and proposed solution.

How to approach each station

  1. Read the problem carefully and identify the symptom.
  2. List the parts of the system that could be involved — hardware, software, or network.
  3. Suggest the simplest checks first before recommending bigger changes.
  4. Propose a final solution — a fix, an upgrade, or a device recommendation.
  5. Explain your reasoning using what you learned in earlier topics.
