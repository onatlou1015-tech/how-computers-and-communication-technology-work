Topic 6

Networks and the Internet

LAN, WAN, Wi-Fi, IP addresses, DNS, packets, and protocols — how devices connect and communicate.

What Is a Network?

A network is a group of two or more devices that are connected together so they can share data, resources, and information. Every time you send a message, watch a video, or open a website, you are using a network. Networks are what make modern communication and the internet possible.

Network: Two or more devices connected together so they can share data and resources. The connections can be wired (using cables) or wireless (using radio waves).

Why Do We Use Networks?

Networks allow devices to do things they could not do alone:

  • Share files and data: Send a document from one computer to another.
  • Share resources: Multiple computers can use the same printer or internet connection.
  • Communicate: Send messages, make calls, and hold video meetings.
  • Access information: Browse websites, watch videos, and use online services.
  • Collaborate: Work on the same document or project from different locations.

Types of Networks

Networks are usually grouped by how big an area they cover. The four most common types are LAN, WAN, Wi-Fi, and mobile networks.

Small area

LAN

Local Area Network. Covers a small area like a home, school, or office. Usually owned and managed by one organisation.

Large area

WAN

Wide Area Network. Covers a large area like a city, country, or the whole world. The internet itself is the largest WAN.

Wireless LAN

Wi-Fi

A wireless LAN. Devices connect to a network using radio waves instead of cables, usually within a building.

Wide coverage

Mobile Networks

Wireless WANs that use cell towers. 3G, 4G, and 5G are generations of mobile technology — each faster than the last.

LAN — Local Area Network

A LAN covers a small area — usually a single building or campus. Think of the network at your school: all the classroom computers, printers, and the school server are connected together in one LAN. A home network, where your laptop, phone, and smart TV share the same Wi-Fi, is also a LAN.

LANs are usually fast, cheap to set up, and owned by a single person or organisation.

WAN — Wide Area Network

A WAN covers a large geographic area — a city, a country, or even the whole world. WANs are often made by connecting many LANs together using long-distance links like fibre optic cables, satellites, or leased phone lines.

The internet itself is the biggest WAN in the world. It is a global network that connects millions of smaller networks.

Wi-Fi — Wireless LAN

Wi-Fi is a way of connecting to a LAN without cables. Instead of plugging in a network cable, your device talks to a wireless access point using radio waves. Wi-Fi is convenient and flexible, but it usually has a shorter range and can be affected by walls and interference.

Mobile Networks — 3G, 4G, 5G

Mobile networks let your phone connect to the internet from almost anywhere. They use a system of cell towers that cover the country in small zones. As you move, your phone automatically connects to the nearest tower.

Mobile networks have improved through generations:

  • 3G: The first generation fast enough for basic video calls and web browsing.
  • 4G: Much faster — good for streaming video and everyday apps.
  • 5G: The newest generation — extremely fast, with very low delay. Perfect for live video and connected devices.

Network Components

Networks are made of several different hardware devices. Each one has a specific job in helping data travel from one place to another. Understanding these components helps you understand how a network works.

Router

Directs data between networks and chooses the best path for packets.

Modem

Converts signals between your home network and your internet provider.

Switch

Connects multiple devices within one LAN and sends data only to the right device.

Access Point

Provides Wi-Fi coverage so wireless devices can join the network.

IP Address

A unique number for each device — like a mailing address on a network.

DNS

Translates website names into IP addresses — like a phonebook for websites.

Router — Directing the Traffic

A router is a device that connects different networks together and directs data between them. When you send a message from your home network to a friend's network, the router decides the best path for the data to take. Routers are like traffic police for data on the internet.

Modem — The Translator

A modem (short for modulator–demodulator) converts signals between your home network and the network of your internet service provider (ISP). It changes the data from the form your home devices use into the form that can travel over telephone lines, cable TV lines, or fibre optic cables.

Many modern home routers include a built-in modem, which is why you may see a single device called a "gateway" or "modem-router".

Switch — The Local Connector

A switch connects multiple devices within a single LAN — like all the computers in a school lab. Unlike older hubs, which broadcast data to every device, a switch sends data only to the device it is meant for. This makes the network faster and more secure.

Access Point — Providing Wi-Fi

A wireless access point lets wireless devices join a wired network. When you connect your phone to your school's Wi-Fi, you are connecting to an access point. The access point passes your data to the rest of the network through a cable.

IP Address — The Mailing Address

Every device on a network has a unique IP address (Internet Protocol address). An IP address is a series of numbers, like 192.168.1.10, that identifies that device on the network. Without an IP address, data would not know where to go — just like a letter without an address cannot be delivered.

DNS — The Internet's Phonebook

It would be difficult to remember the IP address of every website you want to visit. That is why we use DNS — the Domain Name System. DNS is like a phonebook that translates human-friendly names (like www.bbc.co.uk) into the IP addresses that computers use to find each other.

When you type a web address into your browser, your computer automatically asks a DNS server "what is the IP address for this name?" The DNS server replies with the number, and your computer connects to that address.

Analogy: Imagine you want to send a letter to a friend. The IP address is like their home address — it tells the post office exactly where to deliver the letter. The DNS is like a phonebook that lets you look up their address from their name. Without either of them, the letter could not be delivered.

What Is the Internet?

The internet is the largest network in the world. But it is not a single network owned by one company. The internet is a network of networks — millions of separate networks (home networks, school networks, business networks, government networks) all connected together using the same rules.

Internet: A global network of interconnected networks. It is not owned by any one person or organisation. Instead, it works because every network follows the same set of rules — called protocols — so they can all understand each other.

How Big Is the Internet?

The internet connects billions of devices worldwide — phones, computers, servers, smart TVs, and many more. Every time you open a website, send a message, or watch a video, your data travels across many different networks to reach its destination. The internet makes this happen in fractions of a second.

What Is a Server?

A server is a powerful computer that stores information and provides it to other devices when they ask for it. When you visit a website, your computer (called the client) sends a request to the server that holds that website. The server responds by sending the website back.

How Data Travels Across the Internet

Data does not travel across the internet as one big chunk. Instead, it is broken into small pieces called packets. Each packet is sent separately and can take a different path to reach its destination. When all the packets arrive, they are put back together in the correct order.

Packets — Breaking Data Into Pieces

A packet is a small piece of data. When you send a message or load a web page, the data is split into many packets. Each packet contains:

  • A piece of the data itself — part of the message or file.
  • The destination IP address — where the packet is going.
  • The sender's IP address — where the packet came from.
  • A sequence number — so the packets can be put back in order.

Packets travel independently across the network, and each one may take a different route. This system is called packet switching, and it is one of the most important ideas behind the internet.

Example: Sending the message "HELLO"

Original: H E L L O
Split into packets: P1 · H P2 · E P3 · L P4 · L P5 · O
Travel separately: P1 P3 P2 P5 P4
Reassembled: P1 · H P2 · E P3 · L P4 · L P5 · O

Routers — Directing the Traffic

Routers are the devices that move packets across the internet. When a packet arrives at a router, the router looks at the destination IP address and decides which direction to send the packet next. Each router passes the packet on to the next router, until the packet finally reaches its destination.

Because different packets can take different paths, if one route is busy or broken, the packets can travel another way. This makes the internet very reliable.

Protocols — The Rules of the Internet

A protocol is a set of rules that devices follow so they can understand each other. Every device on the internet must follow the same protocols, or communication would be impossible.

Two important protocols you should know:

  • TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol / Internet Protocol): The basic rules that allow data to be sent, addressed, and reassembled across the internet. IP handles the addressing; TCP makes sure all the packets arrive safely and in the correct order.
  • HTTP / HTTPS (HyperText Transfer Protocol / Secure): The rules for sending and receiving web pages. When you see https:// in a web address, it means the connection is secure and your data is encrypted.

Analogy: Think of the postal system. Packets are the individual letters. IP addresses are the addresses written on each envelope. Routers are the postal workers and sorting offices that move letters along the route. Protocols are the rules of the postal system — the standards everyone follows so letters get delivered correctly.

Tracing the Route of a Web Request

When you type a website address into your browser and press Enter, a surprising amount of activity happens in a fraction of a second. Let us trace exactly what happens when you visit a website.

You type: www.example.com

www.example.com
  1. You type the URL. Your browser receives the web address "www.example.com". Your computer does not know where this website lives yet.
  2. DNS lookup. Your computer asks a DNS server: "What is the IP address for www.example.com?" The DNS server is like a phonebook, and it replies with the correct IP address (for example, 93.184.216.34).
  3. Your request is split into packets. The request to load the web page is divided into small packets, each labelled with your IP address and the destination IP address.
  4. Packets travel through routers. The packets pass through many routers on the way. Each router reads the destination address and forwards the packet to the next router along the route.
  5. The server receives the request. The web server at the destination IP address receives the packets, puts them back together, and understands what you are asking for.
  6. The server sends a response. The server sends the web page back to you, again split into packets.
  7. The packets travel back to you. The packets travel back through the routers, possibly taking a different route than on the way there.
  8. Your browser displays the page. Your computer reassembles the packets in the correct order, and your browser draws the web page on your screen. All of this usually takes less than a second.

Why This Matters

Every time you open a website, this entire process happens — usually in under a second. If one of the steps fails (for example, DNS cannot find the address, or a router is offline), the website will not load. This is why understanding the route of a web request is so useful when troubleshooting network problems.

Activity: Simulate and Trace

Activity 1 — Simulate Packet Switching

In this activity, you will act out how data travels across a network using the packet switching method.

  1. Divide the class into small groups. Each group represents a router in the network.
  2. Choose one student to be the sender and another to be the receiver.
  3. The sender writes a short message (like "HELLO") on separate slips of paper — one letter per slip.
  4. Each slip is a packet. Write a number on each slip so it can be put back in order.
  5. The sender passes the packets to different "routers" (groups), which pass them on in different directions.
  6. All packets eventually reach the receiver, who puts them back in the correct order to read the message.
  7. Discuss: what happened if one router was slow or missing? How did packets find another way?
