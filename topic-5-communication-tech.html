Introduction to Communication Technology

Sender, message, channel, receiver — and how communication has evolved from letters to video calls.

What Is Communication Technology?

Communication is the act of sharing information between two or more people. It has always been a basic human need — from the earliest spoken words to handwritten letters, from the invention of the telephone to today's instant video calls. Communication technology is the set of tools, devices, and systems that people use to send and receive information over distance.

Communication technology is any technology that helps people send messages, share information, or exchange ideas across a distance. It includes the devices we use (phones, computers, routers), the networks that carry the signals (cables, radio waves, fibre optics), and the rules that make communication work (protocols).

Communication Then and Now

Communication has changed dramatically in the last 200 years. To understand how powerful modern technology is, it helps to compare it with the past:

  • 200 years ago: The fastest way to send a message over long distances was by letter, carried on horseback, by ship, or by walking messenger. A message could take weeks or months to arrive.
  • 150 years ago: The telegraph made it possible to send coded electrical signals over wires. A message could travel hundreds of kilometres in minutes.
  • 100 years ago: The telephone allowed voice communication over long distances — people could actually hear each other's voices in real time.
  • 50 years ago: Mobile phones and the early internet made it possible to send text messages and connect computers across the world.
  • Today: A single smartphone can send a photo, a video, or a live conversation anywhere on Earth in seconds — often for free.

Think about it: A letter that took six weeks to cross the ocean in 1800 can now be replaced by an instant message that arrives in less than a second — and you can see the other person's face while you send it. That is the power of communication technology.

Why Communication Technology Matters

Communication technology has changed the world in four important ways:

Speed

Messages that once took weeks now arrive in seconds — sometimes instantly.

Distance

We can communicate with anyone anywhere on Earth, no matter how far away.

Cost

Sending a message is far cheaper than in the past — often free with internet access.

Access

Billions of people can now communicate, learn, and work together using the same tools.

Key Components of Communication

Every act of communication — whether it is a face-to-face conversation, a phone call, or a text message — follows the same basic pattern. This pattern is called the SMCR model: Sender → Message → Channel → Receiver. Understanding this model helps you see how all communication technology works.

Sender
The person or device that creates the message.
Message
The information being sent — words, images, sound, or video.
Channel
The medium that carries the message — cable, Wi-Fi, or radio wave.
Receiver
The person or device that gets the message at the end.

1. Sender

The sender is the person, device, or program that creates and sends the message. When you type a text message on your phone, you are the sender. When a computer sends an automatic email, it is the sender.

2. Message

The message is the information being communicated. It can take many forms — written words, spoken voice, a photograph, a video, or even a set of instructions that one computer sends to another.

3. Channel

The channel is the path or medium through which the message travels. The channel is what makes modern communication so powerful — it lets a message travel across a room or across the planet.

Common channels include:

  • Copper cables: Traditional telephone and internet wires.
  • Fibre optic cables: Thin glass strands that carry data using pulses of light — extremely fast.
  • Radio waves: Used for Wi-Fi, mobile networks, Bluetooth, and TV broadcasts.
  • Satellites: Relay signals across continents and oceans.

4. Receiver

The receiver is the person or device that receives the message at the other end. When your friend reads your text message, they are the receiver. When your computer downloads a file from a server, the computer is the receiver.

Putting It Together: A Text Message

Let us trace a simple text message using the SMCR model:

  • Sender: You, typing "See you at 5 PM" on your phone.
  • Message: The text "See you at 5 PM".
  • Channel: Your phone's mobile data or Wi-Fi sends the message to a cell tower, then to the internet, then to your friend's phone.
  • Receiver: Your friend, who sees the message appear on their screen.

Analogy: Think of sending a letter by post. The sender is the person writing the letter. The message is what is written inside. The channel is the postal service — the vans, planes, and mail carriers that move the letter. The receiver is the person whose name is on the envelope. Communication technology simply makes this process happen much faster.

Devices and Transmission Media

Communication technology relies on two things: the devices that send and receive messages, and the transmission media that carry the signals between them. Together they make modern communication possible.

Communication Devices

Communication devices are the hardware that send, receive, or route messages. Some devices are used directly by people (like phones and computers), while others work quietly in the background (like routers and modems).

Phones

Smartphones and mobile phones send voice, text, and video over wireless networks.

Computers

Desktops and laptops connect to the internet to send and receive data of all kinds.

Routers

Routers direct data between devices and connect your home or school to the internet.

Modems & Antennas

Convert signals so they can travel over cables, wireless networks, or satellites.

Transmission Media

Transmission media are the pathways that carry communication signals. They are usually grouped into three main types:

Copper Cables

Older style wires that carry electrical signals. Used in traditional phone lines and older internet connections.

  • Reliable and inexpensive
  • Slower than fibre
  • Can lose signal over long distances

Fibre Optics

Thin strands of glass that carry data as pulses of light. The fastest medium available today.

  • Extremely fast
  • Carries huge amounts of data
  • Not affected by electrical interference

Wireless (Radio Waves)

Signals travel through the air using radio waves — no cables needed. Used in Wi-Fi, mobile networks, and Bluetooth.

  • No wires needed
  • Works over long distances
  • Can be affected by walls and weather

Analog vs. Digital Signals

The messages that travel through transmission media are carried as signals. There are two main types of signals: analog and digital. Understanding the difference helps you see why modern technology is so powerful.

Analog Signals

  • Continuous waves that change smoothly
  • Represented by a smooth curve
  • Used by older telephones and radios
  • Can pick up noise and distortion over distance
Smooth wave

Digital Signals

  • Only two states: 0 (off) or 1 (on)
  • Represented by sharp steps or squares
  • Used by modern phones, computers, and the internet
  • Resistant to noise — signals can be perfectly restored
0 1 1 0 1 0 0 1

Modern communication systems convert analog signals into digital form so that information can be stored, copied, and transmitted without losing quality. This is why a song can be copied a thousand times and still sound identical — digital data stays perfect.

How Communication Has Evolved

The way people communicate has changed more in the last 150 years than in all of human history before that. Let us look at four important stages in that evolution — each one faster, cheaper, and more powerful than the last.

Stage 1 · 1800s and earlier
Letters by Post
Messages were written on paper and physically carried by people on foot, on horseback, on ships, or by train. A letter could take days, weeks, or even months to arrive. Communication was slow and depended on how far the message had to travel.
Stage 2 · Late 1800s to 1900s
Telephone Calls
The telephone allowed people to hear each other's voices in real time over long distances. Calls travelled through copper wires. For the first time, communication was instant — but it required both people to be available at the same moment.
Stage 3 · 1990s to 2000s
Email and Instant Messaging
The internet made it possible to send written messages instantly to anyone with an email address or messenger account. Messages could be sent at any time, and the receiver could read them whenever they were ready. This changed both personal and business communication forever.
Stage 4 · Today
Video Calls and Real-Time Sharing
Modern smartphones and computers allow people to see and hear each other in real time from anywhere in the world. We can share photos, videos, and live video streams in seconds. Communication today is instant, visual, and available almost everywhere.

Method Sender Message Channel Receiver

Telephone
1900s		 The caller Spoken voice (analog signal) Copper telephone wires The person who answers
Email

Video Call
Today		 The caller Live digital audio and video Internet (fibre and wireless) The person on the other screen

Notice the pattern: In every stage, the SMCR structure is the same — there is always a sender, a message, a channel, and a receiver. What changes is the speed, quality, and cost of the communication. As technology improved, messages travelled further, faster, and more cheaply.
