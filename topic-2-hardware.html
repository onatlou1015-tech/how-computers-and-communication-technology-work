Topic 2

Hardware Components

The internal parts that power the computer and the peripheral devices that connect to it.

What Is Hardware?

In Topic 1, you learned that a computer system is made of two essential parts: hardware and software. In this topic, we are going to focus on the hardware — the physical parts that you can see, touch, and hold.

Hardware refers to the physical components of a computer system — the parts you can see and touch. Hardware is divided into two groups: internal components (the parts inside the case) and peripheral components (the parts you connect to the outside).

Every piece of hardware has a specific job to do. Some parts work on data, some parts hold data, some parts bring data in, and some parts send data out. In this topic, you will learn what each component is called, what it does, and how they all work together to make the computer system function.

By the end of this topic, you should be able to look at any computer and identify its main parts. You should also be able to explain what each part does and why it matters.

Internal Components

Internal components are the parts found inside the computer case. They do the main work of the computer. You usually cannot see them unless you open the case, but without them, nothing would work. Let us look at the six most important internal components.

CPU

The "brain" of the computer — processes all instructions and data.

RAM

Short-term memory that holds data while the computer is working.

Storage

Long-term memory (HDD or SSD) that keeps data permanently.

Motherboard

The main circuit board that connects all components together.

Power Supply

Converts electricity from the wall into power the computer can use.

Graphics Card

GPU that handles images, video, and 3D graphics.

1. CPU — Central Processing Unit

The CPU is often called the "brain" of the computer. It is a small chip that carries out the instructions given by programs. Every time you click a button, type a letter, or open an app, the CPU is working behind the scenes to make it happen.

The CPU performs three steps over and over again: it fetches an instruction, decodes what the instruction means, and executes it. This happens billions of times per second. A faster CPU means the computer can do more things at the same time without slowing down.

2. RAM — Random Access Memory

RAM is the computer's short-term memory. It holds the data and programs that are currently in use. When you open an app, it loads into RAM so the CPU can work on it quickly.

RAM is temporary. When you turn off the computer, everything in RAM disappears. This is why you must save your work — if the power goes out before you save, whatever was only in RAM is lost. Having more RAM means you can have more apps open at the same time without the computer slowing down.

3. Storage — HDD and SSD

Storage is the computer's long-term memory. Unlike RAM, storage keeps data even when the computer is turned off. This is where your files, photos, music, and programs live permanently.

There are two main types of storage:

  • HDD (Hard Disk Drive): Uses spinning magnetic disks to store data. It is cheaper and can hold a lot of data, but it is slower and has moving parts.
  • SSD (Solid State Drive): Uses flash memory chips with no moving parts. It is much faster than an HDD but usually more expensive for the same amount of space.

4. Motherboard — The Main Circuit Board

The motherboard is a large circuit board that connects all the other components together. The CPU, RAM, storage drives, and graphics card all plug into the motherboard. It allows them to communicate with each other and share power and data.

Think of the motherboard as the "nervous system" of the computer — it carries signals back and forth between all the parts so that everything works together.

5. Power Supply — The Energy Source

The power supply takes electricity from the wall socket and converts it into the kind of power the computer's internal components need. Without the power supply, no other part would receive electricity, and the computer would not turn on at all.

The power supply also protects the computer from sudden changes in electrical current, such as a spike during a thunderstorm.

6. Graphics Card (GPU) — The Visual Expert

A graphics card, also called a GPU (Graphics Processing Unit), is a special chip that handles everything you see on the screen. It processes images, videos, and 3D graphics much faster than a regular CPU can.

Gamers, video editors, and designers often use powerful graphics cards because they need to render complex visuals quickly. Some computers have the GPU built into the CPU, while others have a separate card that you can upgrade.

Peripheral Components

Peripheral components are the parts you connect to the computer from the outside. They allow you to give data to the computer (input), receive results from it (output), or do both. Peripherals are grouped into three types: input devices, output devices, and devices that are both.

Input Devices

  • Keyboard
  • Mouse
  • Microphone
  • Scanner
  • Camera (webcam)

Output Devices

  • Monitor
  • Printer
  • Speakers
  • Projector

Both Input & Output

  • Touchscreen
  • Headset (with microphone)
  • Touchpad
  • Modem / Network card

Input Devices

Input devices send data into the computer. They are how you tell the computer what you want it to do. Without input devices, the computer would have no instructions and no data to work on.

  • Keyboard: The most common input device. It sends typed letters, numbers, and commands into the computer.
  • Mouse: A pointing device. It sends movement and clicks into the computer, letting you select, drag, and interact with things on the screen.
  • Microphone: Captures sound and sends audio into the computer for recording, calling, or voice commands.
  • Scanner: Reads physical documents and images and turns them into digital files.
  • Camera (Webcam): Captures images and video and sends them to the computer for video calls or recording.

Output Devices

Output devices send data out of the computer to the user. They show you the results of what the computer has processed.

  • Monitor: Displays visual information like text, images, and video.
  • Printer: Produces physical copies of documents and images on paper.
  • Speakers: Output sound — music, voice, notifications, and alerts.
  • Projector: Projects the screen onto a large surface so many people can see it at once.

Devices That Are Both Input and Output

Some peripherals work in both directions. They can send data in and show results out.

  • Touchscreen: Shows images (output) and responds to your touch (input) at the same time.
  • Headset: Has earphones that produce sound (output) and a microphone that captures your voice (input).
  • Touchpad: On laptops, it shows pointer movement (output) while also receiving your finger movements (input).

How They Work Together

On their own, none of these components can do anything useful. They only work when they cooperate. Let us trace what happens when you type a letter on a keyboard and see it appear on a screen.

The Path of Data — Step by Step

  1. Input: You press the "A" key on the keyboard. The keyboard sends a signal to the computer.
  2. Motherboard: The signal travels along the motherboard to reach the CPU.
  3. Process: The CPU receives the signal, decodes it, and decides what character it represents. It then tells the system to display that character.
  4. Storage: If you are typing in a document, the data is temporarily held in RAM while you work, and saved permanently to storage when you save the file.
  5. Output: The GPU sends the image of the letter "A" to the monitor, and you see it appear on the screen.

The Kitchen Analogy

To help you remember how these components work together, imagine a busy kitchen in a restaurant.

Computer Component Kitchen Analogy Why It Matches
CPU The Chef The chef does all the work — chopping, cooking, and preparing dishes. The CPU does all the calculating and processing.
RAM The Countertop The countertop is where the chef puts ingredients while cooking. RAM is where the computer holds data while working. Both are temporary working space.
Storage (HDD/SSD) The Refrigerator The fridge keeps ingredients fresh for a long time. Storage keeps files and programs safe even when the computer is off.
Motherboard Kitchen Counters The counters connect all the areas of the kitchen. The motherboard connects all components so they can share data and power.
Power Supply The Stove / Gas Line The stove provides the energy needed to cook. The power supply provides electricity to run the whole computer.
GPU The Food Plater The plater makes food look beautiful on the plate. The GPU makes images and video look clear and smooth on the screen.

Remember: Just like a kitchen cannot function without a chef, countertop, fridge, and stove all working together, a computer cannot function without all its internal components cooperating. Each one has a special job, and together they make the whole system work.
