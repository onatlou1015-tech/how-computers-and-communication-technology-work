Topic 8 · Final Project

Putting It All Together

Design a complete computer and communication system for a real purpose — and justify every choice you make.

Synthesizing Everything You Have Learned

Throughout this unit, you have learned about every part of a computer and communication system. Now it is time to put all the pieces together. In this final topic, you will design a complete system for a real purpose — choosing the hardware, software, network, and budget that fit the need.

System design means choosing the right combination of hardware, software, networking, and communication tools to solve a specific problem or serve a specific purpose — and being able to justify why each choice was made.

A Quick Recap of the Whole Unit

Before you design your own system, let us review the eight topics of this unit and how they all connect:

1. Computer Systems

Hardware + software working together to input, process, store, and output.

2. Hardware

Internal components (CPU, RAM, storage) and peripherals (input, output).

3. Software

System software (OS, utilities) and application software (apps, tools).

4. How Computers Work

IPO+S model, fetch–decode–execute cycle, binary data.

5. Communication Tech

Sender, message, channel, receiver — and how it all evolved.

6. Networks

LAN, WAN, Wi-Fi, IP addresses, DNS, packets, protocols.

7. Problem Solving

Troubleshooting, choosing devices, recommending upgrades.

8. Design a System

Put it all together for a real purpose — that's what you do now.

Analogy: Designing a computer system is like planning a trip. You need to know where you are going (purpose), what you will carry (hardware and software), how you will get there (network and communication), and how much you can spend (budget). When all four fit together, the trip works.

Choosing Your Design Project

Your design project must serve a specific purpose. Below are four options. Choose one — or, with your teacher's approval, propose your own similar project.

A Home Office

A person working from home who needs to attend video meetings, write documents, and manage their schedule.

Considerations:

  • Reliable internet for video calls
  • Quiet space with good lighting
  • Comfortable desk and screen setup
  • Backup storage for work files

A Small Business

A small shop or café that needs to accept digital payments, track sales, and communicate with customers.

Considerations:

  • Payment terminal and secure connection
  • Point-of-sale software
  • Receipt printing or digital receipts
  • Reliable internet and backup connection

A Classroom

A teacher who needs a system for interactive lessons, presenting material, and allowing students to use devices.

Considerations:

  • Projector or large display
  • Reliable Wi-Fi for student devices
  • Educational software and apps
  • Network security for student access

A Gaming Setup

A person who wants to play modern games online, stream to an audience, and enjoy high-quality graphics and sound.

Considerations:

  • Powerful GPU and CPU
  • Fast, low-latency internet
  • High refresh-rate monitor and headset
  • Cooling and quiet operation

Whatever project you choose, your design must clearly answer the same four questions:

  1. What hardware will you use, and why?
  2. What software is needed, and why?
  3. What network and communication setup is required?
  4. What is the budget, and how does your design fit it?

The Five Design Requirements

Every system design must include five things. Think of them as your design checklist. If your design answers all five, it is complete.

Hardware Components

List the CPU, RAM, storage, motherboard, GPU, and any peripherals. Explain why each one was chosen for this specific purpose.

Software Needed

Choose an operating system, plus any application software. Explain how each program supports the purpose.

Network & Communication

Describe the connection (Wi-Fi, LAN, internet), the devices involved (router, modem, access point), and how data will flow.

Budget Considerations

Estimate the cost of each major part. Make sure the total fits the realistic budget for your chosen project.

How Parts Work Together

Explain how all the parts and programs interact to carry out the purpose — from input to output.

Example: A Simple Home Office System

Below is a short example of how a home office system might be designed. You can use this as a model for your own design.

Example Design: A Home Office System

Purpose
A person working from home who needs to attend video meetings, write documents, manage their schedule, and store work files securely.
Hardware
A mid-range laptop with an Intel Core i5 or AMD Ryzen 5, 16 GB RAM, a 512 GB SSD, a decent webcam, and a full HD monitor. Plus a wireless keyboard and mouse, a headset with microphone, and an external backup drive.
Software
Windows 11 or macOS (operating system). Google Chrome or Microsoft Edge (web). Microsoft Office or Google Workspace (documents, spreadsheets, presentations). Zoom or Google Meet (video calls). A password manager and antivirus utility.
Network
A Wi-Fi router connected to a fibre broadband plan (at least 50 Mbps). Wi-Fi access point in the home office for a strong signal. Wired Ethernet cable for the laptop if possible, for a more stable connection during video calls.
Budget
Laptop $600–$800, monitor $150, peripherals $120, external drive $60, software often included or available free (Google Workspace), internet plan $40–$60 per month.
How It Works
The laptop loads the operating system from the SSD. The user types in the word processor (input), the CPU processes the text, it is stored in RAM while typing and saved to the SSD. Video calls use the webcam (input), the CPU and GPU process the video, and the internet connection carries the audio and video to the other person. The headset handles audio input and output.

Budget Considerations

A good design must be realistic. Even the best system is not useful if it costs more than the person or organisation can afford. This is why the budget is one of the five design requirements.

Three Budget Tiers

Most projects can be designed at three levels: basic, mid-range, and premium. Each tier has its own balance of price and performance.

Basic Tier

Entry Level

The most affordable option — good for simple tasks and light use.

  • Lower-end CPU or modest RAM
  • Smaller SSD or HDD
  • Free or included software
  • Basic Wi-Fi and internet plan
Mid-Range Tier

Best Value

A balance between cost and performance — good for most everyday needs.

  • Mid-range CPU, 8–16 GB RAM
  • 512 GB–1 TB SSD
  • Paid software with free alternatives
  • Reliable router and internet
Premium Tier

High Performance

For demanding tasks that need speed, capacity, and power.

  • High-end CPU and dedicated GPU
  • 16–32 GB RAM or more
  • 1–2 TB NVMe SSD
  • Top-tier network and peripherals

How to Budget Well

A good budget is not just about spending the least — it is about spending smartly. Here are some guiding questions:

  • What is the most demanding task? The system must be powerful enough for this task, but not necessarily more.
  • Which parts matter most for this purpose? A gaming setup needs a strong GPU; a home office needs a good camera and stable connection.
  • Where can you save money? Free software (like Google Workspace or LibreOffice) can replace paid options.
  • Where is it worth spending more? Reliability and warranty are important for a business or school system.

Analogy: Designing a system to a budget is like packing for a trip with a limited suitcase. You cannot take everything — you must choose the items that matter most for the trip. The same is true for a computer system: choose the parts that matter most for the purpose, and save on the rest.

Activity: Group Design Project

Your Task

Work in small groups (3–4 students). Choose one of the four project types from earlier in this topic — or propose your own with your teacher's approval — and design a complete computer and communication system for that purpose.

Step 1 — Plan Your Project

  1. Choose your project type (home office, small business, classroom, gaming, or your own).
  2. Define the purpose clearly — what must the system do?
  3. Identify the main users — one person, several people, customers?
  4. Agree on a realistic budget for the project (from your teacher or your own estimate).

Step 2 — Design Your System

Use the design template below to record your decisions. Fill in each section with your choices and your reasons.

My System Design

Step 3 — Prepare Your Presentation

Your group will present your design to the class in about 5 minutes. Your presentation must cover:

  1. The purpose of your system and who will use it.
  2. The hardware you chose and why each part fits the purpose.
  3. The software you chose and why.
  4. The network and communication setup.
  5. The budget and how your choices fit it.
  6. How all the parts work together to achieve the purpose.

Step 4 — Peer Review

After each group presents, the rest of the class gives feedback. Use the four prompts below to guide your feedback:

Strengths

What did the group do well? Which choices were especially well justified?

Questions

What questions do you have about the design? Is anything unclear?

Suggestions

What could be improved? Are there other parts, software, or approaches worth considering?

Budget Check

Does the design fit the stated budget? Are the choices realistic for the purpose?

Discussion

After all presentations, discuss these questions together:

Which design was the most realistic? Why?
Which design had the best balance between performance and budget?
What was the hardest choice in your own design, and why?
If you were given twice the budget, how would you change your design?

Quick Check

Test your understanding of system design with these questions.

1. What are the five things every system design must include?

2. Why is budget important in system design?

3. A group is designing a system for professional video editing. Which part matters most?

4. What should a system design explain about the network?

5. What is the main purpose of a peer review?

Student Outcome

Students can design a computer and communication system for a real purpose and justify their choices.
